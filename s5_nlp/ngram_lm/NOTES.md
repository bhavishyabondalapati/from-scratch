# n-gram language model, smoothing, perplexity

**Results on tiny-shakespeare** (word level, vocab of 5,632 words seen at least twice, 25k training
lines, 3k test lines; from `shakespeare_perplexity.txt`, regenerated by the slow test):

| model | add-1 | interpolated Kneser-Ney |
|---|---|---|
| unigram | 349 | 347 |
| bigram | 894 | **186** |
| trigram | 2,403 | **183** |

Add-1 smoothing gives so much probability to the ~5,600² unseen word pairs that the bigram model
is *worse* than the unigram. Kneser-Ney uses the extra context properly. On synthetic data drawn
from a known bigram model, the bigram KN model's test perplexity is within 3% of the true model's
(tested).

## Intuition
A language model assigns a probability to the next word given the previous ones. An n-gram model
makes a strong simplification: only the last `n − 1` words matter. Then probabilities are just
ratios of counts: `P(sat | the cat) = count("the cat sat") / count("the cat")`.

The trouble is **sparsity**: most word combinations never appear in training, so their count is 0,
and one zero makes a whole sentence impossible (infinite perplexity). **Smoothing** moves a little
probability mass from seen to unseen events:
- **add-k:** pretend everything was seen k extra times (simple, too crude for large vocabularies);
- **interpolation:** mix the trigram, bigram, and unigram estimates;
- **Kneser-Ney:** subtract a fixed discount from every seen count and give the freed mass to a
  lower-order model that asks "*in how many different contexts* does this word appear?" (so
  "Francisco", which almost only follows "San", is not a good guess after a random word).

**Perplexity** = `exp(average negative log-probability per word)`: roughly "how many words the model
is choosing between". A uniform guess over V words has perplexity exactly V (tested).

## Core math
- MLE: `P(w | h) = c(h, w) / c(h)`.
- add-k: `(c(h, w) + k) / (c(h) + kV)`.
- Interpolation: `Σₘ λₘ P_MLE^{(m)}(w | h)`, λ tuned on held-out data (weight of an unseen context
  is passed to the next order down).
- Interpolated Kneser-Ney: `P(w|h) = max(c(h,w) − d, 0)/c(h) + d·N₁₊(h•)/c(h) · P_lower(w|h')`,
  lower orders use continuation counts `N₁₊(•h'w)`, base case uniform `1/V`.
- Perplexity: `PP = exp(−(1/N) Σ log P(wᵢ | hᵢ))`.

## Complexity
Training: O(tokens · n) counting. Memory: number of distinct n-grams (grows fast with n).
Query: O(n) dictionary lookups.

## Common pitfalls
- Comparing perplexities computed with different vocabularies or `<unk>` handling.
- Forgetting the end-of-sentence token (it is a prediction too) or counting start tokens.
- Probabilities that do not sum to one after "smoothing" (tested for every method here).
- Tuning λ or k on the test set.
- Larger n is not automatically better: data sparsity grows exponentially with n.

## When to use vs. alternatives
- Fast, interpretable baselines; spelling correction, keyboard prediction, speech recognition
  decoding (historically), counting-based features.
- Neural LMs (RNNs, transformers in `s5_nlp/gpt`) generalise across similar contexts and handle long
  history, at much higher compute.

## Interview questions
1. **Define perplexity.** `exp` of the average negative log-likelihood per token; the effective
   branching factor.
2. **Why smooth?** Unseen n-grams get probability 0 under MLE, making test perplexity infinite and
   overfitting the training counts.
3. **Why is add-1 bad for language?** With a large vocabulary it hands most of the probability mass
   to the huge number of unseen n-grams.
4. **What makes Kneser-Ney special?** Absolute discounting plus continuation counts for the
   lower-order distribution.
5. **What is the Markov assumption here?** The next word depends only on the previous `n − 1` words.
